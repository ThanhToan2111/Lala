# Introduction v1

Multimodal learning combines complementary views of the same phenomenon, such as language, audio, and vision. The standard response to this opportunity is to design a richer fusion mechanism: tensor products, cross-modal attention, multiplicative factors, or higher-order alignment objectives. Yet the presence of multiple modalities does not by itself establish that a useful non-additive interaction is present in the learned representations. A model may perform joint computation while relying primarily on one dominant modality, or it may encode a predictive interaction that is unrelated to the downstream task.

This observation creates a conceptual gap before the usual fusion question. Rather than asking only how modalities should be fused, we first ask whether non-additive predictive structure is measurably present and useful under a specified representation and hypothesis class. We study this question through four conditions: cross-modal predictive identifiability, task relevance, representation fidelity, and downstream accessibility. These conditions are related but not equivalent. In particular, measurable predictive interaction need not imply task headroom, and fidelity to an interaction target need not imply that a downstream task learner can use the resulting representation.

Our framework makes these distinctions explicit. Given frozen representations from two source modalities and a target representation, an additive predictor sums unimodal predictions, while a joint predictor may compute a genuinely joint function. We define the empirical joint predictive advantage as the difference between their variance-weighted global R² scores. Under closed nested function spaces and squared risk, the corresponding population quantity is the squared norm of the additional conditional-mean structure accessible in the richer joint space beyond the additive projection. This interpretation is deliberately narrower than PID synergy or a causal interaction: the score is representation-, hypothesis-class-, optimization-, and sample-dependent.

We then separate prediction from representation learning. Joint-Advantage Distillation (JAD) trains an explicit low-rank interaction representation to match the empirical difference between the joint and additive predictors. Fidelity is evaluated by recovering this difference or, where available, a known interaction target. Accessibility is evaluated separately through downstream probes and task headroom. This separation gives a two-gate view of natural screening: a candidate must first exhibit empirical joint predictive advantage and then demonstrate downstream utility. The historical threshold used in earlier experiments is retained only as an operational screen, not treated as a universal significance threshold.

We validate the framework in controlled and natural regimes. Synthetic interaction benchmarks and IPIB provide known structure and non-identifiable controls. Naturalized IPIB preserves frozen MOSEI vision/audio representation geometry while injecting a fixed interaction component with strength β∈{0,0.25,0.5,1}. Across five seeds, the diagnostic responds systematically to the injected interaction strength, and recovery of the known component increases, although JAD recovery remains partial. This establishes construct-validity evidence without claiming that the natural dataset has a known ground-truth interaction.

On natural representations, MOSEI vision/audio-to-text produces a stable positive empirical joint predictive advantage under the frozen Product-Joint protocol. However, the distilled interaction does not produce a stable sentiment accuracy gain over a lower-order representation. MUStARD and MELD do not yield stable positive screens in the tested settings, and a nested residual audit does not reveal substantial additional MELD headroom. A preregistered B=50 alignment-breaking confirmation further finds that the observed MOSEI score is not separated from this operational null (`p_hat=0.098`), motivating a careful distinction between an operational positive and a statistically calibrated discovery.

Our contributions are fourfold:

1. We formalize joint predictive advantage as a population squared-risk projection gap and state the assumptions and non-claims required for interpreting it.
2. We introduce a diagnostic hierarchy that separates cross-modal identifiability, task relevance, interaction fidelity, and downstream accessibility.
3. We provide controlled IPIB and Naturalized IPIB benchmarks for testing detection and recovery when interaction structure is known.
4. We conduct a natural multimodal audit across MOSEI, MUStARD, and MELD, showing that positive predictive interaction does not automatically imply downstream utility and that the historical screening threshold is not universally calibrated.

The resulting message is methodological rather than architectural: multimodal interaction learning should be evidence-driven. Non-additive predictive structure must be distinguished from task relevance, faithful representation, and accessibility before a more complex fusion mechanism is interpreted as useful multimodal reasoning.
